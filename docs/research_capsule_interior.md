# Research: inside the capsule (shot 3.8, and 4.3)

*Research pass, 2026-10-07, for the revision note at 1:11: "It makes sense to show many shots of many astronauts
strapping in. Research what the inside of this particular rocket might look like." Reference images are in
`media/refs/capsule/` (see `SOURCES.md` there).*

## Summary

- **The public record of Mengzhou's cabin is thin.** Its shape, size, crew numbers, escape system, landing system and
  launch pad are documented. I found no official picture or description of a cabin fitted with seats and displays for
  a crew. The cabin in the film therefore has to be designed by analogy with Shenzhou, Orion and Crew Dragon, and this
  file says which parts are fact and which are inference.
- **One finding changes the script.** Mengzhou's capsule flies **without a fairing**: the escape tower is mounted
  directly on the bare capsule. The script's "shroud still over the window" (4.3) and "launch-escape tower and shroud"
  (architecture section) describe a Soyuz, Shenzhou or Apollo arrangement, not this vehicle. Section 8 gives the fix.
- **Crew of eight:** fly them as two launches of four (section 5).
- **The montage:** six shots of about 0.8 s, in real procedural order, mostly hands and hardware (section 7).

### What is not public
I searched in Chinese and English and found nothing official on: the number and layout of seats in the lunar cabin,
the seat design, the displays, the window layout, or a new launch-and-entry pressure suit for Mengzhou. Secondary
Chinese sources say only that the cabin separates crew space from equipment, that the controls were designed for
ergonomics, and that the seats sit on a shock-isolating frame
([Baidu Baike](https://baike.baidu.com/item/%E6%A2%A6%E8%88%9F%E8%BD%BD%E4%BA%BA%E9%A3%9E%E8%88%B9/64088195), not
an official source). The 2020 capsule and the museum model show a few small round ports in the side wall; I could not
confirm which are windows.

This cuts both ways. No reviewer can say a seat or a screen is wrong, but the things that *are* public (no fairing,
the tower, the capsule's blunt cone, the open launch tower, three or seven seats) will be checked.

## 2. The pad and the way in (Wenchang)

- The crewed-lunar pad is the third launch position at Wenchang, first used on 11 February 2026. Its tower is about
  120 m tall, the tallest in China, and **fully open**: it has no rotating service platforms wrapped around the
  rocket, unlike the Shenzhou tower at Jiuquan
  ([Xinhua, 15 Feb 2026](https://www.news.cn/20260215/5108335dfbc74702bf229ae1cb582b21/c.html),
  [Xinhua, 12 Feb 2026](https://www.news.cn/tech/20260212/94c629e312314864bc9db3ebd42c1e84/c.html)).
- A commentary column reports one crew access arm (登船臂) on the east side and one on the west, at different
  heights for the two rocket variants ([The Paper, 16 Aug 2025](https://m.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_31413137)).
  This is a secondary source; the official reports I read do not describe how crews board.
- What it looks like (`01_…`): a pale concrete-and-steel tower with X-braced bays, tall lattice lightning masts on
  either side, palm trees at the foot, the sea beyond. A white rocket with the capsule and its needle-like escape
  tower on top.
- For the film: the crew ride a lift up the tower, walk a short enclosed arm to a small clean room at the capsule's
  side hatch, and board there. That is true of every modern crewed pad and is consistent with what is reported.

## 3. Suits

- **Wangyu (望宇)** is China's *lunar surface* suit, unveiled on 28 September 2024. It is never worn in the capsule
  for launch.
- **For launch and entry** China's crews wear a soft pressure suit closely related to the Russian Sokol: white with
  blue trim, a soft helmet that is part of the suit with a hinged clear visor, metal wrist rings for the gloves,
  hoses and cables that plug in at the belly (`03_…`, `12_…`). I found no public unveiling of a new suit of this kind
  for Mengzhou.
- The film's launch-and-entry suit in `docs/character_bible.md` (pale fabric, azurite panels, wrist rings, a hose
  connector on the torso, a soft hood-helmet with a hinged visor, a blue comms cap) is consistent with this family.
  Keep it. The emblem on the left chest is the round pale-blue-dot patch.
- Two details that come with a soft-helmet suit: the helmet cannot be carried under the arm, so on the walkout it is
  worn thrown back with the visor open; and each crew member carries a portable ventilator, a box the size of a lunch
  box with a hose to the suit, because the suit is airtight and hot.

## 5. Eight crew: the defensible arrangement

**Recommended: two launches of four.** Expedition 1 flies as two crews of four (Flights E1-A and E1-B) on two Long
March 10–class launches in the same lunar window, each meeting its own lander in lunar orbit. The capsule is a
Mengzhou-derived vehicle with four seats.

Why this and not eight in one cabin:

- The real capsule seats 3 for the Moon and at most 7 for low orbit. Four to the Moon is a small stretch (Orion
  carries four); eight is a different, invented vehicle, and a two-tier cabin of 4 + 4 for a four-day flight in about
  13 m³ is exactly the kind of thing a reviewer circles.
- Real stations already do this: crews of six or seven arrive in separate vehicles. It also splits the risk.
- The assignment letter (`docs/research_mission_assignment.md`) already says "launching as two crews of four".

How to seat four: one row of four across the cabin, as in Crew Dragon (`11_…`), heads toward the hatch side, backs to
the heat shield, knees up. The real cabin is wider than Dragon's, so four abreast fits. One wide console of large
touch displays hangs above their faces, with a strip of guarded physical switches below it. Two seats in the middle
for the commander and pilot, mission specialists outboard. Jade is outboard, next to a small round window.

How to shoot it so nobody objects:

- Never show more than four people in one cabin frame, and never a wide shot that implies more seats behind.
- Prefer tight shots: hands, straps, a visor, one shoulder and a patch. This also follows the round's rule about
  avoiding generated faces.
- The montage may show any of the eight. Each of them strapped in the same way, in the same kind of cabin, on their
  own launch. Nothing on screen needs to say there were two launches.
- An assumption to be aware of: the real lander (Lanyue) carries two. A lander for four is the film's fiction, as the
  station is.

## 6. The moments, in order

**Suit-up room.** A bright, plain room with reclining chairs, one per crew member, and a suit technician for each.
The crew wear thin long underwear. The suit goes on legs first; the technician zips and seals it. The gloves go on
last: the hand pushes into the glove until the two metal rings meet at the wrist, then the technician turns the outer
ring a few degrees until a tab snaps over. Then the leak check: the crew member lies back, the visor is closed and
latched, a hose from a wall console is plugged into the port on the belly, and the suit swells until its wrinkles
smooth out and the sleeves go round and stiff. Everyone watches a dial for a minute. The crew member holds still;
movement changes the pressure (`06_…`, `07_…`, `12_…`).

**Walkout.** The crew walk out of the building in line, the commander first, to a waiting van, past cameras and a
cheering crowd behind a barrier (`08_…`). They wave; they do not stop. In China this is a formal send-off: the
commander reports the crew ready and is answered with one word, 出发 ("depart"). With soft-helmet suits the helmets
are open and each person carries a ventilator box.

**Tower, access arm, white room.** A lift to the top of the tower. A short enclosed bridge, loud with wind and
ventilation. At its end, a room hardly bigger than a lift, joined to the capsule's side hatch (`09_…`). Here the
closeout crew work: five or so people in white coveralls with a number on the shoulder, caps, headsets and blue
gloves. NASA's lead calls them "a pit crew". They fit and lock helmets and gloves, make the last suit checks, and
send the crew in one at a time, usually the far seat first.

**Seat ingress.** The hatch is a round-cornered opening at knee to chest height. The crew member sits on the sill,
swings both legs in, and is guided by a hand on the shoulder onto their back in the seat. The seat is a shallow metal
tray with thin pads. The knees come up; the boots go onto a foot rest. From here on the crew member is nearly
helpless and things are done to them.

**Harness.** Five straps of flat webbing, each ending in a flat metal tongue: two over the shoulders, two across the
hips, one up between the legs. They meet at a round buckle the size of a jam-jar lid on the lower belly (`10_…`).
Each tongue is pushed into its own slot in the rim of the buckle and seats with a click. Then the helper tightens:
hip straps first, by pulling their loose tails outward, then the shoulder straps, by hauling the tails down toward the
buckle. This is done hard, with body weight; the crew member is jolted down into the seat and grunts. The face of the
buckle is a knob: a quarter turn releases all five straps at once, so no hand goes near it again until orbit. On
Orion two people do this for each seat, a suit technician and an astronaut from the support team; "each seat includes
five straps". Soyuz and Shenzhou add a strap across the knees.

**Umbilical.** A bundle of hoses and cables in a white sleeve comes from the side wall to the suit (`04_…`): breathing
air or oxygen, ventilation, the communications cable, sometimes a medical sensor cable. Each hose has a metal collar
that is pushed onto the suit port and turned until it locks; the helper tugs it once to prove it. The suit stirs as
cool air starts to flow. A voice check follows, each crew member in turn.

**Hatch close.** The closeout crew back out. Two of them clean the hatch seals with a wipe and inspect them with a
torch: "even a single strand of hair" on a seal is a problem. The hatch swings shut and is latched from outside. From
inside, the bright disc of the white room narrows and goes out, a handle turns, and the cabin is suddenly quiet
except for fans.

**Visor down and leak check.** Gloves on and locked. Each crew member swings the visor down and latches it. The suit
is pressurised for a minute and the filling is timed; in a Soyuz the crew member turns a blue valve on the chest to
do it, and the gloves stay on from then until orbit. The visor is then usually reopened until the last minutes before
launch, when it comes down for the ascent.

**The window.** On this vehicle the window is not covered. Before launch it shows the tower's steelwork a few metres
away, floodlit, and then the access arm swinging clear. This is where "visor reflection of the tower" comes from, and
it is correct.

Sources for this section: NASA, ["Get In, We're Going to the Moon: Meet NASA's Artemis Closeout Crew"](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/kennedy/get-in-were-going-to-the-moon-meet-nasas-artemis-closeout-crew);
NASA, [Orion crew systems](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/crew-systems/); Samantha Cristoforetti's logbooks for ESA
([strapping in](https://outpost42.esa.int/blog/diario-di-bordo/single/l200-logbook-part-4-strapped-in-soyuz/),
[the Sokol suit](https://outpost42.esa.int/blog/diario-di-bordo/single/l-34-our-sokol-to-go-to-space/)); launch-day
coverage of Artemis II ([Starlust](https://starlust.org/nas-as-artemis-ii-crew-arrives-at-launch-pad-and-boards-orion-spacecraft-ahead-of-launch),
[SpaceQ](https://spaceq.ca/artemis-2-crew-and-teams-conduct-a-countdown-demonstration-test/)). The order of small
steps differs between vehicles; the order above is common to all of them. The exact buckle hardware on Orion and on
Chinese seats is not published; the round rotary buckle is the type shown in `10_…` and used on the Shuttle.

### The photograph of her children
Plausible, with conditions. Crews do take family pictures: a NASA photograph from shuttle flight STS-56 shows the
middeck lockers covered in them, and Soyuz and Dragon crews hang a small toy chosen by their children in front of the
panel to show when weightlessness begins. But nothing loose is allowed at launch, and nothing may cover a display, a
switch or a label. So:

- a small laminated print, about 5 × 7 cm;
- held flat by a hook-and-loop dot or a strip of amber polyimide tape (the tape that holds everything in a cabin);
- on a blank patch of structure at the outboard edge of the console, low and to her side, or on the frame beside her
  window. Not on a screen and not among switches;
- she looks at it with her eyes only. Strapped in, with the visor down, the head barely turns.

An equally true alternative is the clear pocket of the checklist strapped to her thigh, which is where a glance
naturally falls.

## 8. Flags for the script

1. **No shroud on this vehicle.** For a Mengzhou-like capsule, change "launch-escape tower and shroud" to
   "launch-escape tower mounted on the bare capsule (no fairing)", and change 4.3 from "the shroud still over the
   window". The honest replacement is better: the launch is before dawn, and a rocket climbing east out of Wenchang
   rises into sunlight within the first minutes. At 我思念 the window can be indigo and then flood with low gold
   light across her visor. If the director prefers a dark, windowless cabin that suddenly fills with light when a
   fairing is thrown off (two and a half to three and a half minutes into a Soyuz or Shenzhou launch), that is a real
   and powerful beat, but it belongs to those vehicles, and the script should then stop calling the capsule
   Mengzhou-like on this point.
2. **Jade's shots are hands and three-quarter back** (shots 5 and 6), which keeps to the rule on her likeness.
3. **Two launches of four** should be added to the "Vehicle & mission architecture" section so that 3.8, the letter in
   3.5 and the name cards in 5.3 agree.
4. **The capsule lands on land** under three parachutes with airbags in the real design; a sea landing has so far
   only been done in the abort test. `docs/audit_claude.md` already raised this for 8.1.
